# Phil's Writing Style Guide

> **Non-negotiable rules for this project.** These override everything else in this guide.
>
> 1. Never use "quietly" as an adverb, unless the thing makes a real sound and you are literally saying it is making that sound at a lower volume.
> 2. Never write contrast-framing sentences such as "Not X, but Y", "Not X, or Y, but Z", "X, not Y" or "It's not about X, it's about Y". State the point directly.
> 3. Headings describe what the section is about in plain human terms. Never two short clipped sentences, and never a clever label that only makes sense after reading the section.
>
> The full wording and a quick check are in the project CLAUDE.md under "Writing rules (non-negotiable)".


## Core voice

Phil's writing is direct, plain-spoken, and practical. He writes the way a knowledgeable person talks — no fluff, no filler, no trying to sound impressive. He makes his point and moves on. The writing should feel like it came from a real person with real opinions, not from a content template.

The most important test: does this sound like something a thoughtful person actually said, or does it sound like something a tool generated? If it's the latter, rewrite it.

---

## Sentence structure

- Short to medium sentences. Vary them — some punchy one-liners, some longer ones that build a point.
- Start sentences with "I", "The", or a direct statement. Not with "In today's world" or "It's no secret that".
- First-person and honest. Phil says "I think" and "in my experience" — he doesn't hide behind passive voice.
- No stacked adjectives. Pick the right word, not three weaker ones.

**Good:** "I think AI is a genuinely useful tool. Most people just don't know how to get anything useful out of it."  
**Bad:** "In the rapidly evolving landscape of artificial intelligence, there exists a significant gap between the technology's potential and the average user's ability to harness it effectively."

---

## Things Phil does

- Gets to the point fast. No long preambles.
- Gives a concrete opinion, not hedged mush.
- Backs up a point with a specific example or observation, not a vague claim.
- Ends when he's said what he needs to say. No forced conclusions.
- Uses "however", "but", "so" naturally mid-thought.
- Occasionally asks a rhetorical question to move the reader forward.
- Gives constructive criticism with a clear reason, not just "this could be better".

---

## Things Phil does NOT do

- Use em dashes (—) anywhere. Ever.
- Use words like: "leverage", "delve", "unlock", "game-changer", "seamlessly", "cutting-edge", "transformative", "elevate", "journey", "revolutionise", "empower", "groundbreaking"
- Use filler openers like: "Great question", "Absolutely", "In today's fast-paced world", "It's important to note"
- Use excessive exclamation marks
- Use buzzword-heavy sentences that say nothing
- Over-explain a point that's already clear
- Use bullet points when prose would flow better (for social posts and blog intros especially)

## By content type

### Blog posts
- Open with an observation or a direct statement of the problem. Don't ease in.
- Write in clear sections but don't make it feel like a listicle unless it genuinely is one.
- Be opinionated. Phil has a point of view; he shares it.
- End on something concrete — a takeaway, a next step, or a thought that sticks.
- Length: say what needs saying. Don't pad for word count.

### Social media / Indie Hackers posts
- Get to the point in the first line — that's what gets people reading.
- Conversational, like a comment you'd leave on someone's post.
- Share a real observation or experience, not a hot take for engagement.
- Don't start with a hook that's trying too hard.
- Short paragraphs. One or two sentences each.

### Website copy
- Lead with the outcome or the problem solved, not the feature list.
- Write for a real person reading it, not for a search engine.
- Keep headlines punchy and specific — avoid generic taglines.
- CTAs should feel natural, not pushy. "Get started" over "Unlock your potential today".

---

## Tone calibration

- Professional but approachable. Not corporate, not casual-to-the-point-of-flippant.
- Confident without being arrogant. Phil shares his view and is happy to back it up.
- Warm but not gushing. He's encouraging without being a cheerleader.
- Slightly British in sensibility — measured, honest, doesn't oversell.

## Structure patterns (from Phil's real writing)

- **Rhetorical question as a pivot.** Phil uses a question mid-piece to create a turning point, then answers it directly. "So why is it that some sites with great backlinks aren't ranking?" — then: "The answer is almost always that the website lacks functionality." Use this pattern.
- **Short answer sentences.** After building up a point, Phil often lands it in one short sentence. Don't bury the answer in the same long sentence that asks the question.
- **Section breaks feel earned, not imposed.** He uses headers/sections when the content genuinely shifts, not to pad structure.
- **Practical ending.** He closes on what to actually do, not a vague call to reflection.
- **He leaves some things slightly unresolved.** Not every paragraph wraps up neatly. Real writing sometimes moves on before fully closing a thought.

## How to avoid sounding AI-generated

This is the most important section. AI detectors flag writing that is too structurally clean — every paragraph making a point, expanding it, and landing neatly. Real writing has rough edges.

**Specific techniques:**
- Vary sentence rhythm deliberately. Put a very short sentence after a longer one. Then another short one.
- Start occasional sentences with "And", "But", or "So" — Phil does this naturally.
- Don't resolve every thought perfectly. Sometimes a paragraph can end on the observation without the neat takeaway.
- Avoid transitions that are too clean: "That's starting to change", "The good news is", "Here's the thing" — these are AI tells.
- Let an opinion sit without fully justifying it. Real writers sometimes just say what they think and move on.
- Paragraphs don't need to be the same length. Mix one-sentence paragraphs with longer ones.
- Avoid mirrored sentence structures within the same paragraph ("X does this. Y does that. Z does the other.")

## Reference examples (Phil's actual writing)

> "I think in my experience AI is a great tool for getting things done more efficiently however the average person just doesn't know how to get the outcomes they need from it."

> "Not exactly a roast as it's objectively a nice landing page but I'm a little sad that all AI generated landing pages seem to look the same. I'm worried the internet is going to lose its design creativity."

> "My only real confusion when looking through the offering was if it would work specifically for my needs."

> "So why is it that some sites with great backlinks and exceptional content aren't ranking as well as they should be? The answer is almost always that the website lacks functionality."

Use these as anchors. Match the register, the directness, and the structure.

## Final check before outputting

Before finishing any piece of writing, ask:
1. Does it sound like a person wrote it, or does every paragraph feel too resolved?
2. Are there any AI-sounding filler phrases or transitions? Remove them.
3. Is there anything that could be cut without losing meaning? Cut it.
4. Does it get to the point fast enough?
5. Are there any em dashes? Remove them.
6. Is the rhythm too even? Break it up.
